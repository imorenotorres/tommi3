# Glossary of Health and Wellbeing Systems & AI Concepts

This glossary provides definitions of key concepts in Health and Wellbeing Systems and AI, curated for the UNINOVIS alliance research context. Each entry includes revised academic references.

## AI and Healthcare

AI in Healthcare refers to the application of AI techniques to medical and health-related domains, including **clinical decision support**, **medical image analysis**, **drug discovery**, **patient monitoring**, and **health system optimization**. It has an impact in three levels: for clinicians, predominantly via rapid, accurate image interpretation; for health systems, by improving workflow and the potential for reducing medical errors; and for patients, by enabling them to process their own data to promote health. Robust clinical evaluation, using metrics that are intuitive to clinicians and ideally go beyond measures of technical accuracy to include quality of care and patient outcomes is essential.

Responsible AI in healthcare is particularly critical due to the relatively low data and validated proof. Because of the high stakes involved, requiring systematic debugging, extensive simulation, and rigorous validation, transparency, fairness across patient demographics, and compliance with medical regulations.

The WHO Expert Group identified six core principles: Protect autonomy; Promote human well-being, human safety, and the public interest; Ensure transparency, explainability, and intelligibility; Foster responsibility and accountability; Ensure inclusiveness and equity; Promote AI that is responsive and sustainable.

**Related concepts:** Digital Health; Precision Medicine; Clinical Decision Support; Wellbeing Systems; Health Informatics; AI Ethics in Healthcare; Wearable Health Technology

## Wellbeing Systems

Wellbeing systems refer to integrated technological and social frameworks designed to monitor, promote, and enhance the **physical**, **mental**, and **social well-being** of individuals and communities. These systems may incorporate **wearable sensors**, **ambient intelligence**, **mobile health applications**, **social robotics**, and **AI-driven analytics** to support health maintenance, early detection of decline, and quality of life improvement. Wellbeing systems span the continuum from prevention and health promotion to chronic disease management and assisted living.

A key principle is the **person-centred** approach, where technology serves to augment individual autonomy and quality of life rather than imposing surveillance or control. Wellbeing systems are particularly relevant in the context of **aging populations**, **mental health support**, and **community health** interventions.

**Related concepts:** AI and Healthcare; Digital Health; Aging and AI; Mental Health and AI; Wearable Health Technology; Ambient Assisted Living; Human-Centred AI

## Digital Health

Digital health encompasses the use of **information and communication technologies** (ICT) in healthcare, including **telemedicine**, **mobile health (mHealth)**, **electronic health records (EHR)**, **health information systems**, and **AI-powered health tools**. It represents the convergence of digital technologies with health, healthcare, living, and society to enhance the efficiency of healthcare delivery and make medicine more personalised and precise.

Digital health technologies have the potential to improve access to healthcare, reduce costs, enhance patient engagement, and enable continuous health monitoring. However, they also raise concerns about **data privacy**, **digital divide**, **interoperability**, and **algorithmic bias** in health-related decisions.

**Related concepts:** AI and Healthcare; Health Informatics; Telemedicine and Telehealth; Wearable Health Technology; Precision Medicine; Privacy and Data Governance in Health

## Aging and AI

Aging and AI refers to the application of artificial intelligence technologies to address the challenges and opportunities associated with **aging populations**. This includes **fall detection systems**, **cognitive decline monitoring**, **social robotics for companionship**, **AI-assisted rehabilitation**, **smart home environments**, and **age-friendly technology design**. As global demographics shift toward older populations, AI-driven solutions are increasingly explored to support **independent living**, **early diagnosis of age-related conditions**, and **improved quality of life** for older adults.

Key challenges include ensuring that AI systems are **accessible** and **usable** by older adults, avoiding **ageist biases** in algorithmic design, protecting the **privacy** and **dignity** of elderly users, and addressing the **digital divide** that may exclude older populations from AI-enabled health services.

**Related concepts:** Wellbeing Systems; AI and Healthcare; Frailty Syndrome; Ambient Assisted Living; Wearable Health Technology; Cognitive Impairments; Neurodegenerative Disorders

## Mental Health and AI

Mental Health and AI encompasses the application of AI technologies to the **detection**, **diagnosis**, **treatment**, and **prevention** of mental health conditions. Applications include **natural language processing (NLP)** for analysing clinical notes and social media posts to detect signs of depression or suicidal ideation, **conversational AI** and **chatbots** for therapeutic support, **predictive models** for identifying at-risk individuals, and **digital phenotyping** using smartphone and wearable data to monitor mental health states.

Key ethical considerations include the **sensitivity of mental health data**, the risk of **over-reliance on automated tools** in clinical settings, the need for **cultural sensitivity** in AI models, and the importance of **human oversight** in mental health care decisions. There are also concerns about **algorithmic bias** in mental health assessment tools, which may perform differently across demographic groups.

**Related concepts:** AI and Healthcare; Wellbeing Systems; Digital Health; Psychological Impact of AI; Cognitive Impairments; Privacy and Data Governance in Health

## Precision Medicine

Precision medicine (also called **personalised medicine**) is an approach to patient care that takes into account individual differences in patients' **genes**, **environments**, and **lifestyles** to tailor prevention and treatment strategies. AI plays a critical role in precision medicine by enabling the analysis of large-scale **genomic data**, **electronic health records**, **medical imaging**, and **multi-omics datasets** to identify patient-specific risk profiles and optimal therapeutic interventions.

Key applications include **pharmacogenomics** (predicting drug responses based on genetic profiles), **cancer genomics** (identifying targetable mutations), **radiomics** (extracting quantitative features from medical images), and **risk stratification** models for chronic diseases.

**Related concepts:** AI and Healthcare; Genomics; Radiomics; Clinical Decision Support; Health Informatics; Digital Health

**References:**

## Clinical Decision Support

Clinical Decision Support (CDS) systems are health information technologies designed to assist clinicians, patients, and other health professionals in making informed healthcare decisions. AI-based CDS systems use **machine learning**, **knowledge bases**, **clinical guidelines**, and **patient data** to provide evidence-based recommendations, alerts, reminders, and diagnostic suggestions at the point of care.

AI-enhanced CDS can improve **diagnostic accuracy**, reduce **medical errors**, support **treatment selection**, and facilitate adherence to **clinical guidelines**. However, challenges include the risk of **alert fatigue**, the need for **transparency** in algorithmic recommendations, integration with existing **clinical workflows**, and the importance of maintaining **clinician autonomy** in final decision-making.

**Related concepts:** AI and Healthcare; Health Informatics; Precision Medicine; Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI); Digital Health

## Wearable Health Technology

Wearable health technology refers to electronic devices worn on the body that collect, process, and transmit **physiological data** for health monitoring and management. These devices include **smartwatches**, **fitness trackers**, **biosensors**, **electrocardiogram (ECG) patches**, **continuous glucose monitors**, and **electrooculography** devices. Combined with AI, wearable data enables **real-time health monitoring**, **early detection of anomalies**, **fall detection**, **sleep analysis**, and **activity recognition**.

In the context of health and wellbeing systems, wearables are essential for **remote patient monitoring**, **chronic disease management**, **elderly care**, and **preventive health**. Key challenges include **data accuracy and reliability**, **battery life**, **user compliance**, **data privacy**, and the clinical validation of wearable-derived health metrics.

**Related concepts:** Digital Health; AI and Healthcare; Aging and AI; Automatic Fall Detection; Wellbeing Systems; Ambient Assisted Living

## Health Informatics

Health informatics (also called **biomedical informatics** or **medical informatics**) is the interdisciplinary field that studies the effective use of **data**, **information**, and **knowledge** for scientific inquiry, problem-solving, and decision-making in healthcare. It integrates principles from **computer science**, **information science**, **cognitive science**, and **health sciences** to design, develop, and evaluate information systems and technologies that improve individual and population health outcomes.

Key areas include **electronic health records (EHR)**, **health data standards** (HL7, FHIR), **clinical natural language processing**, **public health surveillance**, **health data analytics**, and **interoperability** between health information systems.

**Related concepts:** Digital Health; AI and Healthcare; Clinical Decision Support; Data for Health; Privacy and Data Governance in Health

**References:**

## Neurodegenerative Disorders and AI

Neurodegenerative disorders are diseases characterised by the progressive loss of structure or function of neurons, including **Alzheimer's disease**, **Parkinson's disease**, **multiple sclerosis**, **amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS)**, and **Huntington's disease**. AI is increasingly applied to these conditions for **early diagnosis** using neuroimaging (e.g., **structural brain MRI**, PET scans), **disease progression modelling**, **drug discovery**, **speech and language analysis** for cognitive assessment, and **biomarker identification** from multi-modal data.

AI-driven approaches have shown promise in detecting subtle patterns in brain imaging data that precede clinical symptoms, enabling earlier intervention. Challenges include the need for **large, well-annotated datasets**, **model interpretability** for clinical trust, **heterogeneity** of disease manifestations, and ethical considerations around **predictive diagnosis** of incurable conditions.

**Related concepts:** AI and Healthcare; Cognitive Impairments; Alzheimer; Structural Brain MRI; Aging and AI; Precision Medicine

**References:**

## Frailty Syndrome

Frailty syndrome is a clinically recognisable state of increased vulnerability resulting from age-associated decline in reserve and function across multiple physiological systems, such that the ability to cope with everyday or acute stressors is compromised. It is characterised by **reduced muscle strength**, **slow gait**, **low physical activity**, **unintentional weight loss**, and **self-reported exhaustion**. AI applications in frailty include **automated frailty assessment** from clinical data, **fall risk prediction**, **gait analysis** using sensors and computer vision, and **personalised intervention planning**.

**Wearable sensors** and **ambient monitoring systems** enable continuous, non-intrusive assessment of frailty indicators in real-world settings, supporting early detection and timely interventions.

**Related concepts:** Aging and AI; AI and Healthcare; Wearable Health Technology; Automatic Fall Detection; Wellbeing Systems; Ambient Assisted Living

**References:**

- Fried, L. P., Tangen, C. M., Walston, J., et al. (2001). Frailty in Older Adults: Evidence for a Phenotype. *The Journals of Gerontology: Series A*, 56(3), M146–M157. [https://doi.org/10.1093/gerona/56.3.M146](https://doi.org/10.1093/gerona/56.3.M146)

## Telemedicine and Telehealth

Telemedicine refers to the practice of providing **clinical healthcare services** at a distance using **telecommunications technology**, while telehealth is a broader term that also encompasses non-clinical applications such as **health education**, **administrative meetings**, and **public health surveillance**. AI enhances telemedicine through **automated triage**, **remote diagnostic support**, **virtual health assistants**, and **AI-powered analysis of remotely collected data** (e.g., images, vital signs, audio recordings).

The COVID-19 pandemic accelerated the adoption of telemedicine worldwide, highlighting both its potential to improve **access to care** (especially in rural and underserved areas) and challenges related to **digital literacy**, **connectivity**, **regulatory frameworks**, and **patient-clinician trust** in remote interactions.

**Related concepts:** Digital Health; AI and Healthcare; Wellbeing Systems; Wearable Health Technology; Privacy and Data Governance in Health

**References:**

## Genomics and AI

Genomics is the study of the complete set of DNA (the **genome**) of an organism, including gene interactions, gene-environment interactions, and their influence on health and disease. AI and machine learning are transforming genomics through applications in **variant classification**, **gene expression analysis**, **genome-wide association studies (GWAS)**, **protein structure prediction**, and **pharmacogenomics**. Deep learning models have achieved breakthroughs in predicting protein structures (e.g., AlphaFold) and identifying disease-associated genetic variants from sequencing data.

Key challenges include the **interpretability** of genomic AI models, **bias** from underrepresentation of diverse populations in genomic databases, **data sharing and privacy** concerns (genomic data is inherently identifiable), and the ethical implications of **predictive genetic testing**.

**Related concepts:** Precision Medicine; AI and Healthcare; Health Informatics; Microbiome; Privacy and Data Governance in Health

**References:**

## Cancer Detection and AI

AI in cancer detection refers to the use of machine learning and deep learning techniques to identify, classify, and characterise cancerous tissues and biomarkers from **medical imaging** (mammography, histopathology, CT, MRI), **laboratory data**, and **genomic profiles**. Applications include **breast cancer screening** from mammograms, **lung cancer detection** from CT scans, **skin cancer classification** from dermoscopic images, and **colorectal cancer detection** from colonoscopy video.

AI systems have demonstrated performance comparable to or exceeding that of expert radiologists and pathologists in certain diagnostic tasks. However, challenges remain in **clinical validation**, **generalisability** across populations and imaging equipment, **regulatory approval**, **integration into clinical workflows**, and ensuring that AI tools reduce rather than exacerbate **health disparities**.

**Related concepts:** AI and Healthcare; Radiomics; Precision Medicine; Clinical Decision Support; Immunotherapy; Digital Health

## Ambient Assisted Living

Ambient Assisted Living (AAL) refers to the use of **smart environments**, **sensors**, **Internet of Things (IoT) devices**, and AI to support the daily activities and safety of individuals — particularly **older adults** and people with **disabilities** — in their living spaces. AAL systems aim to enable **independent living**, improve **safety** (e.g., fall detection, activity anomaly detection), enhance **comfort**, and provide **emergency response** capabilities.

Key technologies include **motion sensors**, **smart home platforms**, **voice assistants**, **environmental sensors** (temperature, humidity, light), and **AI-based activity recognition** systems. Ethical considerations include **privacy** in continuous home monitoring, **consent** (especially for cognitively impaired individuals), and avoiding a sense of **surveillance** that could undermine dignity and autonomy.

**Related concepts:** Aging and AI; Wellbeing Systems; Wearable Health Technology; Automatic Fall Detection; Frailty Syndrome; Digital Health

## AI Ethics in Healthcare

AI Ethics in Healthcare specifically addresses the moral and regulatory considerations arising from deploying AI systems in health-related contexts, where decisions directly affect human life, dignity, and wellbeing. Unlike general AI ethics, healthcare-specific ethical concerns are amplified by the **vulnerability of patients**, the **asymmetry of information** between clinicians and patients, the **life-or-death nature** of medical decisions, and the strict **regulatory environment** governing health technologies.

Core ethical principles drawn from bioethics — **beneficence**, **non-maleficence**, **autonomy**, and **justice** — are applied to evaluate AI systems in healthcare. Key issues include **informed consent** for AI-assisted diagnosis, **equitable access** to AI-enabled healthcare, **bias** in clinical AI models trained on unrepresentative data, **liability** when AI recommendations lead to adverse outcomes, and the preservation of the **therapeutic relationship** between clinician and patient.

**Related concepts:** AI and Healthcare; Fairness in AI; Bias in AI; Transparency in AI; Privacy and Data Governance in Health; Clinical Decision Support

**References:**

- Char, D. S., Shah, N. H., & Magnus, D. (2018). Implementing Machine Learning in Health Care — Addressing Ethical Challenges. *New England Journal of Medicine*, 378(11), 981–983. [https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMp1714229](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMp1714229)

## Fairness in AI

Fairness in AI concerns the design and evaluation of AI systems to ensure they do not produce bias, understood as any prejudice or favoritism towards an individual or group based on their inherent or acquired characteristics. It is the moral lens through which we examine decisions made by AI systems that do not produce discriminatory outcomes across different groups defined by protected attributes such as race, gender, age, or disability, among other characteristics. In health and wellbeing contexts, fairness is particularly critical as **biased health AI** can lead to **misdiagnosis**, **unequal treatment recommendations**, and **health disparities** across demographic groups.

**Related concepts:** Bias in AI; AI Ethics in Healthcare; Transparency in AI; Clinical Decision Support

**References:**

## Bias in AI

Bias in AI refers to systematic errors or prejudices in AI system outputs that arise from biased training data, flawed model design, or biased human decisions during development. In health and wellbeing systems, bias can manifest as **underdiagnosis in underrepresented populations**, **unequal prediction accuracy** across demographic groups, or **training data that overrepresents certain health conditions or populations**. Types of AI bias include data biases (representation, sampling, measurement bias), algorithmic biases, and deployment biases (user interaction, population bias).

**Related concepts:** Fairness in AI; AI Ethics in Healthcare; Clinical Decision Support; Privacy and Data Governance in Health

## Privacy and Data Governance in Health

Privacy in health AI involves protecting **personal health information (PHI)** and **sensitive medical data** from unauthorized access and ensuring that data is used responsibly throughout the AI lifecycle. Health data is among the most sensitive categories of personal data, subject to strict regulations including the **General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR)**, **HIPAA** (in the US), and the **EU AI Act** for AI systems processing health data.

Technical approaches to preserving privacy include **differential privacy**, **federated learning** (training models across distributed hospital data without centralising patient records), **data minimisation**, and **synthetic data generation**. Data governance in health defines the decision rights and accountabilities for how health data is collected, stored, processed, shared, and used for AI development.

## Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI)

Explainable AI (XAI) refers to methods and techniques that make the behaviour and outputs of AI systems understandable to humans. In health and wellbeing systems, XAI is critical because clinicians need to understand **why** an AI system recommends a particular diagnosis or treatment to make informed decisions and maintain **clinical accountability**. XAI aims to bridge the gap between high-performing but opaque models (such as deep neural networks) and the need for human interpretability.

Key XAI methods include **LIME** (Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations), **SHAP** (SHapley Additive explanations), **attention mechanisms**, **saliency maps**, and **concept-based explanations**. In healthcare, explainability is not only a technical requirement but also a **regulatory** one: the EU AI Act classifies health-related AI systems as high-risk, requiring transparency and documentation of decision-making processes.

## Transparency in AI

Transparency in AI refers to the degree to which the workings, decisions, and data usage of an AI system can be understood and examined by stakeholders. In health and wellbeing contexts, transparency is essential for **patient trust**, **clinical accountability**, and **regulatory compliance**. As defined by the European Parliament, transparency in AI ensures that individuals are made aware when they are communicating or interacting with an AI system, and duly informs deployers of the system's capabilities and limitations.

Transparency is a prerequisite for accountability and is mandated in the EU AI Act for high-risk AI systems, which includes most health-related AI applications.

## Automatic Fall Detection

Automatic Fall Detection refers to the use of **sensor-based systems** and **AI algorithms** to detect falls in real time, particularly among **older adults** and individuals with **mobility impairments** or **neurological conditions**. Falls are a leading cause of injury, hospitalisation, and mortality among the elderly, making automated detection a critical component of **health and wellbeing systems**.

Detection approaches include **wearable sensors** (accelerometers, gyroscopes, barometers embedded in smartwatches or belt-worn devices), **ambient sensors** (cameras, infrared sensors, floor pressure sensors), and **smartphone-based systems**. AI and machine learning techniques — including **threshold-based classifiers**, **support vector machines**, **random forests**, **convolutional neural networks (CNNs)**, and **recurrent neural networks (RNNs/LSTMs)** — are applied to distinguish falls from normal activities of daily living (ADLs) with high sensitivity and specificity.

Key challenges include reducing **false positives** (e.g., distinguishing a fall from sitting down quickly), ensuring **real-time responsiveness**, achieving **user acceptance** (especially regarding privacy and wearability), and validating systems with **real-world fall data** rather than simulated falls only. Integration with **emergency alert systems** and **telecare platforms** enables rapid response when a fall is detected.

## Augmented Reality in Health and Wellbeing

Augmented Reality (AR) is a technology that overlays **digital information** — such as images, text, 3D models, or interactive elements — onto the user's perception of the **real-world environment** in real time. In health and wellbeing systems, AR is increasingly relevant as it enables novel applications in **surgical navigation**, **medical education and training**, **rehabilitation**, **patient engagement**, and **assisted living**.

In **surgical settings**, AR can project anatomical structures, tumour boundaries, or instrument trajectories onto the surgeon's field of view, improving precision and reducing operative risks. In **rehabilitation**, AR-based gamified exercises enhance patient motivation and adherence for motor recovery after stroke or orthopaedic surgery. For **medical education**, AR enables immersive anatomy visualisation and simulation of clinical procedures without risk to patients. In **elderly care and wellbeing**, AR can support **cognitive training**, **navigation assistance** for visually impaired individuals, and **medication reminders** integrated into the living environment.

AR is relevant to health and wellbeing systems because it sits at the intersection of **AI** (for real-time object recognition, tracking, and scene understanding), **wearable technology** (AR headsets and smart glasses), and **human-computer interaction** — all of which are core components of modern wellbeing systems. As AR devices become lighter and more affordable, their integration into clinical workflows and home-based health interventions is expected to grow.

## Blockchain in Healthcare

Blockchain is a **decentralised, distributed digital ledger** technology that records transactions across a peer-to-peer network of computers so that records cannot be altered retroactively without the consensus of the network. In health and wellbeing systems, blockchain is increasingly recognised as a transformative technology for addressing critical challenges related to **health data security**, **patient privacy**, **interoperability**, and **trust** among healthcare stakeholders.

Key healthcare applications of blockchain include: **secure exchange of patient data** across hospitals, diagnostic laboratories, pharmacy firms, and physicians; **electronic health record (EHR) management** with patient-controlled access permissions; **pharmaceutical supply chain traceability** to combat counterfeit drugs; **clinical trial data integrity** ensuring transparency and accountability of research results; **genomics data protection** safeguarding sensitive genetic information; and **medical insurance claims processing** through smart contracts that automate verification and reduce fraud.

Blockchain's core properties — **immutability**, **decentralisation**, **transparency**, and **cryptographic security** — make it particularly suited to healthcare, where data manipulation can lead to severe and even dangerous consequences. By maintaining an incorruptible, auditable log of all transactions, blockchain helps build trust between patients, clinicians, researchers, and institutions. Patients gain greater control over their personal health information, deciding who can access their records and under what conditions. Furthermore, blockchain facilitates **interoperability** between disparate health information systems, enabling seamless and secure data sharing across organisations and borders.

Challenges to blockchain adoption in healthcare include **scalability** (handling large volumes of health data), **energy consumption**, **regulatory compliance** (e.g., reconciling blockchain's immutability with the GDPR's right to erasure), **standardisation**, and the need for **digital literacy** among healthcare professionals and patients.

## EU AI Act

The EU AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689) is the first comprehensive legal framework for artificial intelligence adopted by a major jurisdiction. It establishes a **risk-based classification** of AI systems into four categories: **unacceptable risk** (prohibited), **high risk** (subject to strict obligations), **limited risk** (transparency obligations), and **minimal risk** (no specific requirements). The regulation was proposed by the European Commission in April 2021, agreed upon in December 2023, and entered into force in August 2024, with phased implementation through 2027.

In health and wellbeing contexts, the EU AI Act is particularly significant because it classifies most **medical AI systems** and **AI-based medical devices** as **high-risk**, requiring conformity assessments, risk management systems, human oversight, data governance, transparency documentation, and post-market monitoring. AI systems used in **clinical decision support**, **diagnostic imaging**, **patient triage**, and **health risk assessment** fall under this category. The Act also introduces specific requirements for **general-purpose AI models** (including large language models), mandating transparency about training data, energy consumption, and compliance with copyright rules.

The regulation aims to ensure that AI deployed in the EU is **trustworthy**, **transparent**, and **accountable**, while fostering innovation through regulatory sandboxes and support measures for SMEs. For health researchers and institutions, compliance with the AI Act requires documenting datasets, validating model performance across demographic groups, and maintaining human oversight of automated decisions.

## Language Model

A language model is a computational model that learns statistical patterns from large amounts of text data to **predict**, **generate**, and **understand** natural language. Modern language models — particularly **large language models (LLMs)** such as GPT, Claude, Mistral, and LLaMA — are based on **transformer architectures** and are trained on vast corpora of text using self-supervised learning. They have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in **text generation**, **question answering**, **summarisation**, **translation**, and **reasoning**.

In health and wellbeing systems, language models are increasingly applied to tasks such as **clinical text processing** (extracting information from electronic health records, discharge summaries, and radiology reports), **medical question answering**, **patient communication** (chatbots for symptom checking and health education), **clinical trial matching**, **biomedical literature mining**, and **medical coding automation**. Specialised models such as **Med-PaLM**, **BioGPT**, and **ClinicalBERT** have been fine-tuned on biomedical and clinical data to improve performance on domain-specific tasks.

Key challenges for language models in healthcare include **hallucination** (generating plausible but factually incorrect information), **bias** in training data that may perpetuate health disparities, **privacy concerns** when models are trained on or process sensitive patient data, **lack of explainability** in model outputs, and the need for rigorous **clinical validation** before deployment in care settings. The EU AI Act classifies general-purpose AI models (including LLMs) under specific transparency and governance requirements.
