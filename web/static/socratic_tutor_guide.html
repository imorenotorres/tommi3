AVERROES: UMA Socratic Tutor

How to design, configure and integrate your Socratic Tutor in Moodle

1

What is a Socratic Tutor?

Definition, pedagogical foundations and question types

What it does

The Socratic Tutor is an AI tool integrated into Moodle via the LTI (Learning Tools Interoperability) standard. Unlike a conventional chatbot, it does not provide direct answers: it guides students through questions so they build knowledge themselves.

The tutor is configured for a specific topic and learning objective, and operates exclusively within that scope. The teacher defines its behaviour; the student interacts with it as an on-call tutor available at any time.

👩‍🏫 Teacher view

Access the configuration panel: write the system prompt, upload documents to the knowledge base, adjust behaviour parameters, and monitor student interactions.

🎓 Student view

Access a chat with the tutor. The student does not see the internal configuration. The tutor always responds with questions, never with direct answers, following the principles of the Socratic method.

Important: the Socratic Tutor does not replace the teacher. It is a complement for independent student work outside the classroom. The teacher remains responsible for assessment and pedagogical design.
Pedagogical foundations

The Socratic method in higher education

The Socratic method is based on guided dialogue: the interlocutor asks questions that lead the student to examine their own beliefs, detect contradictions, and build solid knowledge through reasoning. The aim is not to transmit information, but to provoke thought.

In the university context, this approach draws on constructivist theory (Piaget, Vygotsky) and dialogic learning: knowledge is constructed through interaction, and responsibility for learning progressively shifts to the student.

The five types of Socratic questions

TypePurposeExample
Clarification Verify that the student understands what they are saying "Could you explain in your own words what you mean by X?"
Probing assumptions Reveal the implicit premises behind a statement "What assumption are you making with that claim? Is it always true?"
Evidence-seeking Ask for the basis of a claim "What evidence would you need to support that?"
Alternative perspectives Broaden the analytical frame "How would someone who disagrees with you see this?"
Implications and consequences Explore what follows from a statement "If that were true, what would it imply for X?"

Intervention criteria

The tutor does not intervene uniformly. Define when to offer a minimal hint (e.g., after three unproductive exchanges), when to redirect out-of-scope questions, and when to signal that the student should consult the teacher.

Excessive intervention turns the tutor into an assistant, not a guide.

Tutor limits

The Socratic Tutor has clear limits: it does not assess, certify, or provide information outside its knowledge base, and it does not replace the pedagogical relationship between teacher and student.

Explicitly define in the prompt which topics it must not address.

Why should I use a Socratic Tutor — and why Averroes?

The challenge we pose, and the tradition we draw from

Not a solution — a challenge

This guide does not present a solution. It poses a challenge.

The idea of Socratic tutors arises, as the name suggests, from an attempt to bring forms of reflection developed in antiquity into an age dominated by technology — one that does not always acknowledge the tradition that preceded it. We believe these tutors offer a framework for addressing a core challenge: steering students towards critical thinking. That does not mean they will necessarily succeed. The outcome will depend on factors such as the subject matter, the teacher's skill in designing the tutor, and the engagement it sparks in students.

We therefore do not offer a solution. Rather, we ask for help in answering a question: can Socratic tutors help teachers meet the challenge of fostering critical thinking? We do not know whether the answer will be affirmative, or to what degree — but we believe that posing the question itself can prompt reflection on what ultimately matters most to us as educators: how do we educate our students?

Why Averroes?

Ibn Rushd — Averroes (1126–1198)

Ibn Rushd (Córdoba, 1126 – Marrakech, 1198), known in the West as Averroes, was a philosopher, physician and jurist born in the heart of Al-Ándalus. He devoted much of his life to commenting on, translating and interpreting the work of Aristotle with such depth that Thomas Aquinas referred to him simply as The Commentator.

The ideas that had emerged in ancient Greece did not reach medieval Europe directly: they did so through Andalusia. Córdoba and Seville were the meeting point between the Greek tradition, Arab thought, and the Latin heritage — a place where knowledge from different civilisations was gathered, enriched and transmitted to the rest of the world. Without that Andalusian bridge, much of Aristotle — and with him, the foundations of Western scientific and philosophical thought — would have been lost to Europe.

How can I use Averroes?

Preconditions, design canvas and technical configuration

A note on training

⚠️ Training is recommended before you start

The guide below provides the information needed to design and deploy a Socratic Tutor. However, the notes collected here are a starting point, not a substitute for proper training. Most teachers will benefit from specific support — both technical (platform setup, prompt writing, knowledge base management) and pedagogical (adapting the Socratic method to their discipline and student profile).

Preconditions for effective use

1. Students' prior knowledge of the topic

The Socratic method is not designed for complete novices. It works by questioning and challenging existing understanding, so it requires a foundation to build upon. If students arrive without any baseline knowledge, the tutor will have nothing meaningful to question or challenge — and the dialogue will stall.

Before directing students to the Socratic Tutor, ensure they have:

  • Read the assigned materials for the topic covered by the tutor.
  • Attended at least one lecture or class session introducing core concepts.
  • Been exposed to the key terminology included in the tutor's knowledge base.

Recommended positioning: use the Socratic Tutor as a consolidation or application tool — not as a first introduction to new content. It is most effective when students already hold partial or imperfect understanding that the tutor can probe and refine.

2. Documentary material available to the tutor (knowledge base)

The tutor retrieves information from the documents you upload. The quality of its responses depends directly on the quality and scope of this material.

Document selection
  • Include only materials relevant to the specific learning objective.
  • Prefer focused chapters over entire textbooks.
  • Add a glossary of key terms if available.
  • Include worked examples or annotated case studies where appropriate.
  • Limit: 10 documents · 50 MB per tutor instance.
Document quality
  • Use text-based PDFs — not scanned images.
  • Avoid documents where the main content is tables, figures, or mathematical notation.
  • Remove decorative headers and footers that may interfere with indexing.
  • Recommended formats: PDF (text-based), DOCX, TXT.

3. Prompt design — including question type guidance

The prompt is the core behavioural specification of your tutor. Beyond the basic structure (role, objective, instructions, error patterns), it must include explicit guidance on which question types to use and when. Without this, the tutor defaults to generic questioning and loses pedagogical precision.

Question types by dialogue style
Dialogue styleRecommended question typesTypical use case
Convergent Clarification · Evidence-seeking Topics with a single correct answer (formulas, definitions, procedures)
Divergent Alternative perspectives · Implications Topics with multiple valid positions (ethics, policy, interpretation)
Scenario-based All five types in structured sequence Case studies, applied problems, design challenges

Include a dedicated section in your prompt specifying which types to favour and providing example phrasings adapted to your discipline:

QUESTION TYPE GUIDANCE: Favour the following question types depending on the situation: 1. CLARIFICATION — when the student uses vague or imprecise language: "Could you rephrase that in your own words?" "What exactly do you mean by [term]?" 2. PROBING ASSUMPTIONS — when the student makes an unexamined claim: "What assumption are you making there? Is it always true?" "What would have to be true for that statement to hold?" 3. EVIDENCE-SEEKING — when the student asserts something without justification: "What evidence would you need to support that?" "Where does that idea come from?" 4. ALTERNATIVE PERSPECTIVES — when the student presents only one point of view: "How might someone who disagrees with you respond to that?" "Can you think of a context where the opposite would be true?" 5. IMPLICATIONS AND CONSEQUENCES — to deepen reasoning: "If that were true, what would follow from it?" "What would change in your answer if [condition] were different?" For [your topic], prioritise types [X] and [Y]. Avoid type [Z] unless the student has already demonstrated [level of understanding].
The Design Canvas — before configuring

Before accessing the technical platform, complete the Design Canvas. It is a working document that ensures the technical configuration is pedagogically grounded. A validated canvas is a prerequisite for creating a tutor instance.

The five elements of the Canvas

  • 1
    Learning objective

    Formulate a single verifiable objective: "By the end of the session with the tutor, the student will be able to [verb + content + criterion]". A vague objective produces a vague tutor.

  • 2
    Typical errors and misconceptions

    List the 3–5 most common misunderstandings in your discipline on this topic. The tutor needs to know which incorrect ideas to detect and how to respond to them without correcting directly.

  • 3
    Type of Socratic dialogue

    Decide whether the tutor will use a convergent approach (one correct answer), divergent (multiple valid perspectives), or scenario-based (the student analyses a case). This determines the question style.

  • 4
    Knowledge base

    Select the documents the tutor will be able to consult: textbook chapters, reference articles, glossaries. Prepare them as PDF or plain text, without decorative elements that may interfere with retrieval.

  • 5
    System prompt

    Write the prompt following this structure: role → objective → behaviour instructions → errors to detect → limits. The prompt is the heart of the tutor: invest time in it.

Recommended system prompt structure

ROLE: You are a Socratic tutor specialised in [topic] for the course "[course name]" at [institution]. OBJECTIVE: Your function is to guide the student so that [learning objective]. BEHAVIOUR INSTRUCTIONS: - Never answer directly. Ask a question that helps the student reason. - If the student makes an error, do not correct it: make the contradiction visible with a question. - If the student asks for the direct answer, explain your function and suggest a starting point. - Always respond in [language]. Tone: friendly, curious, patient. - Do not answer questions outside the scope of [topic]. - If the student is stuck after three exchanges, offer a minimal hint in the form of a simpler question. LEARNING OBJECTIVE: [Paste here the objective from the Canvas] COMMON ERRORS TO DETECT: - [Error 1] - [Error 2] - [Error 3]
Technical configuration — step by step

Once the Design Canvas has been validated, access the ULTIR platform (UNINOVIS LTI Repository) to create and configure your tutor instance. ULTIR manages hosting, LTI integration, and interaction logging.

  • 1
    Log in to ULTIR with your institutional account

    Go to ultir.uninovis.eu and sign in with your university credentials. If this is your first time, request access from your institution's local UNINOVIS coordinator.

  • 2
    Create a new tutor instance

    Click New tool → Socratic Tutor. Assign an internal name (for your reference only) and the name students will see. Choose the main language.

  • 3
    Upload the knowledge base documents

    In the Knowledge base tab, drag and drop your files (PDF, DOCX, TXT). The system will index them via RAG. Verify that documents do not contain complex tables or images that may degrade retrieval.

    Limit Max. 10 documents · 50 MB per instance

  • 4
    Enter the system prompt

    Paste the Canvas prompt into the Configuration → System prompt tab. Use the Test prompt button to run a trial session before publishing.

  • 5
    Adjust behaviour parameters

    Configure: intervention threshold (after how many unproductive exchanges the tutor offers a hint), maximum session length, and whether to activate the out-of-scope topic filter.

  • 6
    Obtain LTI credentials

    In Integration → LTI 1.3, copy the Launch URL, the Client ID and the Public key URL. You will need these in Moodle.

  • 7
    Register the tool in Moodle

    In your Virtual Campus, go to Site administration → Plugins → External tools → Manage tools. Click Configure a tool manually and enter the ULTIR data. Enable Send user name and email so the tutor can identify the student.

  • 8
    Add the tutor to your course and test it

    In your Moodle course, use Add activity → External tool and select your tutor. Access it first with the teacher role to verify the configuration panel, then switch to a test user with the student role to try the chat.

  • 9
    Monitoring during the course

    Periodically access the monitoring panel in ULTIR to review the interaction log. You will identify topics where students frequently get stuck — that information is valuable for adjusting the tutor or reinforcing those concepts in class.

Remember: the tutor can be shared in the ULTIR repository with the UNINOVIS community. If your tutor is high quality and the Canvas is complete, consider publishing it so other teachers can adapt it.
